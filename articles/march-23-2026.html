Nubank Eyes the U.S., BNPL Surges, and Mexico's Open Finance Stalls

A week of landmark moves: digital lenders push into new markets while regulators struggle to keep pace with innovation across the region.

🏦
Expansion · Brazil / USA / Mexico
Nubank Secures U.S. National Bank Charter and Activates Mexican Banking License
With 131 million customers across Latin America, Nu Holdings is executing a bold dual-front expansion — activating its full banking status in Mexico while moving to plant a flag in the United States market.
Read more ›

In January 2026, Nu Holdings received conditional approval from the U.S. Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) for the formation of Nubank, N.A., a de novo national bank. Once fully operational, this charter will allow Nu to offer deposit accounts, credit cards, lending products, and digital asset custody under a comprehensive federal framework — a direct challenge to established U.S. digital banks such as Chime, SoFi, and traditional incumbents. Nubank has made clear it intends to apply the same lean, AI-first operating model that powered its LatAm growth.

Simultaneously, Nu Mexico is completing its conversion from a Popular Financial Society (SOFIPO) into a fully licensed bank following CNBV approval in April 2025. The company surpassed 10 million Mexican customers and $4.5 billion USD in deposits by end of 2024, and the full banking license — expected to be operational in 2026 — will allow it to expand its lending portfolio, offer more competitive deposit rates, and compete head-to-head with traditional Mexican banks. Nubank ended 2025 with 131 million total customers, adding 17 million net new accounts in the year at an 83% activity rate.

Relevance for Prosperas: Nubank's Mexico upgrade validates the mass-market digital lending thesis in a country where half of adults remain underserved by formal credit. As Nubank expands its product suite in Mexico, it simultaneously raises both customer expectations and competitive intensity — creating pressure on all credit providers to sharpen underwriting, pricing, and UX for the exact customer segments Prosperas targets.

Market Trend · LatAm
LatAm BNPL Market Set to Hit $18.5 Billion in 2026, Expanding Into Physical Retail
Buy Now, Pay Later is no longer just an e-commerce feature in Latin America — it is moving aggressively into in-store and omnichannel environments, with the regional market on track for 24.8% annual growth this year.
Read more ›

New market research confirms that the Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) sector in Latin America is projected to reach $18.5 billion in 2026, up from $14.9 billion in 2025 — representing a 24.8% annual increase. The sector is forecast to grow at a compound annual rate of 18.3% through 2031, potentially reaching $43 billion. The most significant structural shift is the migration of BNPL from pure digital checkout flows into physical retail settings, with merchants in furniture, fashion, electronics, and homeware integrating installment payment options at the point of sale.

Brazil and Mexico are leading this expansion, each building on different cultural foundations. In Brazil, BNPL taps into the deeply established "parcelado" (installment) habit, with providers like Mercado Pago and Nubank embedding pay-later logic directly into digital wallets and Pix-adjacent payment flows. In Argentina, BNPL is used as a hedge against persistent inflation — customers prefer installments to preserve purchasing power. In Colombia, players like Addi — which closed a new debt round in late February 2026 — are penetrating physical retail channels rapidly. Competitive differentiation is increasingly shifting toward underwriting quality, multi-channel merchant integration, and regulatory compliance as consumer protection rules tighten.

Relevance for Prosperas: BNPL's expansion into physical retail represents a major access channel for underserved consumers — particularly women in informal markets who transact primarily in-person. As BNPL matures, the ability to build credit history from these transactions becomes critical for providers like Prosperas that want to graduate customers from micro-credit into richer financial products.

Funding · Colombia
Kala Raises $55M in Debt to Expand Credit Access for Colombia's Underserved
Colombian fintech Kala has secured $55 million in debt financing to scale credit services for lower-income populations, including customers aged up to 90 — groups systematically excluded by traditional lenders.
Read more ›

Kala, a Colombian credit fintech, has closed a $55 million debt round to expand its nationwide lending operations. The company specifically targets consumers in Colombia's socioeconomic strata 2 and 3 — lower-middle income households that typically lack formal employment contracts, payroll history, or traditional credit bureau records. Kala's model is built on alternative data underwriting and community-based distribution, enabling it to extend credit to populations that major banks routinely decline, including elderly borrowers up to age 90. This round follows a $4 million seed round in 2023, marking a dramatic scaling of the company's ambitions.

The funding arrives in a broader context where Colombia's fintech ecosystem is maturing rapidly. Addi, the BNPL and point-of-sale lender, also completed a debt round in late February 2026, reinforcing that debt capital — rather than equity — is now the primary fuel for LatAm credit fintechs reaching scale. Debt funding is particularly well-suited to lending businesses because it can be deployed directly as loan capital, improving unit economics without diluting founders. Alive Ventures also recently raised $55 million for its second impact fund covering Colombia and Peru, with 40% earmarked for fintechs. The volume of credit-focused capital flowing into Colombia signals growing institutional confidence in the country's underserved consumer credit opportunity.

Relevance for Prosperas: Kala's fundraise validates the investment thesis for credit products targeting strata 2 and 3 — the same demographic core that Prosperas serves in Mexico. The deal demonstrates that development finance institutions and institutional debt providers are increasingly willing to back impact-oriented lenders with rigorous underwriting methodologies, which is highly relevant to Prosperas's own capital strategy.

Regulation · Mexico
Mexico's Open Finance Stalls as Fintechs File Constitutional Challenge Against Regulators
Eight years after Mexico's pioneering Fintech Law, key Open Finance regulations remain unpublished — and in January 2026, frustrated industry players took the unprecedented step of suing the CNBV, Banxico, and SHCP for regulatory inaction.
Read more ›

Mexico was a regional pioneer when it passed its Fintech Law in 2018, mandating Open Banking frameworks that would allow third-party access to financial data with customer consent. Yet as of February 2026, the key secondary regulations governing datos transaccionales and datos agregados remain unpublished — more than 2,170 days past their statutory deadline. In January 2026, a coalition of fintech entrepreneurs filed an amparo (constitutional injunction) against the National Banking and Securities Commission (CNBV), the Bank of Mexico (Banxico), and the Ministry of Finance (SHCP), alleging that regulatory inaction constitutes a violation of the constitutional right to conduct economic activity.

The regulatory vacuum has created a two-speed market. Traditional banks have little incentive to build data-sharing infrastructure when there is no legal mandate compelling it. Meanwhile, fintechs like Finerio Connect — which raised $6.5 million to build open finance platforms and now partners with over 120 financial institutions — are constructing private interoperability layers that replicate what Open Finance should formally deliver. Industry observers warn that continued delay cedes competitive ground to Brazil, which has deployed a sophisticated, Central Bank-mandated Open Finance ecosystem with over 42 million active user consents. A potential "Fintech Law 2.0" is being discussed, but no timeline has been announced.

Relevance for Prosperas: Open Finance in Mexico would allow lenders like Prosperas to access verified transaction history and account data from incumbent banks — dramatically improving credit risk assessment for thin-file borrowers, particularly women in the informal economy. Every year of regulatory delay is a year where alternative data approaches must do the heavy lifting that a functional Open Finance framework would provide structurally.

Regulation · Brazil
Brazil Tightens Pix and Open Finance Rules as Adaptation Period Closes
Brazil's Central Bank is enforcing a series of critical regulatory deadlines this spring, formalizing who can participate in Pix, mandating new cybersecurity standards, and finalizing the Pix Parcelado framework — reshaping the competitive landscape for digital lenders.
Read more ›

Brazil continues to set the pace for financial regulation in Latin America. This month marks an important inflection: the Central Bank of Brazil (BCB) is closing the Pix Parcelado adaptation period — a structured installment layer built on top of Pix — during which it has been gathering market feedback and refining technical standards. Pix now processes over 90% of digital transactions in Brazil, and Pix Parcelado represents an attempt to formalize the "parcelado" installment habit within that infrastructure rather than leaving it to private BNPL operators. Simultaneously, non-authorized payment institutions must apply for formal BCB accreditation to operate within the Pix network by May 1, 2026 — a measure designed to tighten oversight of a previously loosely supervised segment.

The Central Bank has also imposed stricter cybersecurity requirements following a 2025 incident in which a Pix intermediary breach resulted in over $130 million in losses. All regulated institutions were required to achieve full compliance with the new rules by March 1, 2026. Brazil's Open Finance system, meanwhile, has reached maturity at scale: more than 42 million user consents are active, billions of API calls are processed across the ecosystem annually, and the Financial-grade API (FAPI) security standard is now industry baseline. This positions Brazil not just as a domestic market leader, but as a reference model for other LatAm regulators to follow — or risk falling further behind.

Relevance for Prosperas: Brazil's regulatory evolution serves as a forward-looking roadmap for Mexico and Colombia. The formalization of Pix Parcelado illustrates how installment credit can be embedded directly into payment infrastructure — reducing friction and expanding access simultaneously. Prosperas should monitor how lenders integrate with Pix Parcelado natively, as Mexico's eventual Open Finance rollout may create analogous embedded credit opportunities through SPEI or CoDi-like rails.

People & Operations · Mexico
Kueski Hires Ex-Nubank, Robinhood Executive as VP of Fraud — Signaling Institutional Maturity
Mexico's leading consumer lender and BNPL platform appointed a seasoned fraud risk executive from top global fintechs, a move that reflects the growing sophistication of the sector's risk infrastructure as credit volumes scale.
Read more ›

In February 2026, Kueski announced the appointment of Pablo Baragiola as Vice President of Fraud. Baragiola brings over a decade of experience in consumer finance, payments, and fraud risk management from leadership roles at Robinhood, Nubank, and Capital One — three of the most sophisticated digital financial institutions globally. Kueski, which operates the number-one finance app on Android in Mexico with nearly one million downloads per month and has issued approximately 40 million loans to date, is scaling both its consumer credit and BNPL products aggressively, making fraud prevention a strategic priority rather than a compliance afterthought.

The hire reflects a broader maturation trend across LatAm fintech: as platforms move from startup mode into institutional-grade operations, the talent profile they recruit shifts decisively toward seasoned operators from global firms. Fraud risk is particularly acute in high-volume unsecured consumer lending — the space Kueski dominates in Mexico — where identity fraud, synthetic identities, and loan stacking can erode portfolio quality rapidly if underwriting and monitoring systems are not enterprise-grade. The appointment also signals competitive intent: Kueski is positioning itself not just as Mexico's leading BNPL provider, but as a full-stack consumer lender capable of competing with traditional banks and the growing digital bank segment.

Relevance for Prosperas: Kueski's investment in fraud infrastructure underscores a reality all credit fintechs serving underbanked markets must confront: as loan volume scales, so does fraud exposure. For Prosperas, this is a prompt to assess whether current fraud detection and identity verification capabilities are proportional to growth ambitions — and whether partnerships with fraud-specialized vendors or data providers should be prioritized in the near term.
